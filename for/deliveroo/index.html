Hi Deliveroo team, I've built you a stop in my city. Your Homepage team decides what each customer sees and why, across three brands and dozens of markets. I've made that call on a smaller stage. At GMNG, a real-money gaming app I scaled to 1M+ users, I redesigned the homepage and first-time experience after talking to players about their pain points, and satisfaction rose 20% in an in-app survey. Below is how I read your homepage today, and what I'd build first.

  1. What I looked at

    Your posting; your web homepage in Soho and in Croydon, logged out, on Sunday 8 November 2026 at 3:30pm and again at 4:17pm; DoorDash Engineering's posts on its GenAI homepage carousels and on carrying restaurant history into grocery and retail; your May 2026 Bundles and top-up launch; and DoorDash's Q3 2026 earnings call. I haven't seen the signed-in app or any data, so this is an outside read and a plan to test, not a verdict.

  2. The first question your homepage asks

    Before any restaurant, your homepage asks what you're here for. On the web it asks with a strip of 95 shortcuts: cuisines, deals, shops, and moments such as Cold and flu, Office lunch and Breakfast. About 16 fit on a laptop screen. The strip moves with the clock: at 3:30pm on a Sunday it ran Supermarket, Burgers, All offers, Reservations, Cold and flu, Breakfast; by 4:17pm Pizza and Chinese had moved up and Breakfast had left the first screen. But it moved the same way in Soho and in Croydon: the first 90 tiles matched at 3:30pm, and the first 16 matched again at 4:17pm. In Soho, eleven tiles are deals or offers, among them both "Top deals" and "Top Deals!". Desserts appears three times under the same name, and each opens something different: dessert restaurants, sweet dishes, and dessert items. The carousels below do follow the place. Croydon got Great value for money, Top-rated deals and Offers near you; Soho got Top picks in your neighbourhood. So the strip follows the clock, the carousels follow the place, and logged out, neither knows the person. Signed in, the app may already do more, and that's the first thing I'd check. The question underneath is the one your posting asks: on this visit, what is this customer here for? Dinner tonight, a top-up shop, a gift, something for a cold. Each mission leads to a different vertical, and the homepage has to recognise it on the first screen.

  3. What I'd build first

    Count before building. For each market and brand, I'd trace how homepage visits start (a tile, a carousel, search or the banner), which mission they end in, and where they end without an order. That sets the order of the roadmap. Then three changes. Rank the strip for each customer, not only the clock. It's already on the page and already moves with the hour, so this is the cheapest end-to-end change with the widest reach: add what this customer orders, the area and the verticals they use, so a weekday lunchtime near offices can lead with Office lunch and a Sunday evening with dinner. One name, one place: merge the three Desserts tiles and the overlapping deal tiles into one clear path each, because tiles customers can't tell apart cost a tap and a doubt. Then test the strip grouped by mission against today's single row. And keep dietary tiles true. Halal, Gluten free and Vegan are promises, so whatever ranks or writes these tiles takes dietary claims only from merchant data, never from a model's guess. I hold the same line in Lará, my AI hotel app, where allergy alerts never depend on the AI. I'd prototype each change with Claude Code on sample data before engineers build it.

  4. From restaurant regular to high-street shopper

    Your May launches set the direction: Bundles lets a customer order from up to three shops with no extra delivery fee, and the top-up lets them add from a nearby shop within ten minutes of a restaurant order. The aim, in your own words, is a trip to the shops on the local high street. The hard case is the customer who has only ever ordered dinner. DoorDash's research calls it a behavioural silo: a long restaurant history, but a stranger to grocery and retail. Its answer was to infer grocery tastes from restaurant orders with an LLM, which improved ranking, including for customers new to those verticals. I'd use that in the moments you already have: after a restaurant order, the homepage shows the top-up window with items that suit the meal; for a customer who orders dinner every Sunday, Sunday's homepage can offer the weekly shop next to it. I've made this jump before. At GMNG, poker was one of our highest-revenue games, but players weren't playing it. I aimed a 7-day onboarding loop at high-paying players from our other games, and 60% of that cohort converted, bringing in $40,000. The test here is a first shop order from restaurant-only customers against a holdout, with no drop in their restaurant orders.

  5. Global stack, local homepage

    DoorDash expects its single tech stack, Deliveroo included, to be done by the first half of 2027, so what ships on it can ship in every market. That brings capabilities such as DoorDash's GenAI homepage carousels to your markets: titles written for each customer and part of the day, checked by three LLMs where any one can veto, which raised the share of relevant stores in the top ten from 68% to 85%, and lifted click rate by double digits in early tests in San Francisco and Manhattan. Bringing that to Deliveroo's markets is more than translation. Titles need the French, Italian, Dutch and Arabic a local would write; the dish knowledge has to know a Sunday roast from a shawarma wrap; and in the UAE and Kuwait, meal times move to iftar and suhoor during Ramadan. I'd agree with the DoorDash and Wolt teams which parts stay global, ship the global version in each market against a holdout, and keep a local change only where that market's result asks for it. That's the line between global work and EMEA problems worth solving properly. I learned why the hard way: at GMNG I launched card payments because my friends and I paid by card, then found 80% of the players who did deposit used Paytm, and moved the gateway to it.

  6. When goals pull apart

    Many teams want the same homepage slots: the customer's next order, deals and partner programmes such as Nectar Prices, new restaurants that need a first order, grocery and retail growth, and ads. I'd make one rule the default and agree it with each team: a placement has to be relevant to this customer to show at all, and each business goal gets a stated share of slots, reviewed every month against what it did to orders. DoorDash's carousel ranking already works this way: its score multiplies engagement by relevance, so a store has to do well on both. When two goals still clash, I'd make the call on the customer's next order and show both teams the numbers behind it. I've worked between paid placements and the people who see them: at XY ADS I built a self-service ad exchange architected for 100M+ monthly impressions, and moved clients from campaigns we ran for them by hand to running their own.

  7. How I'd measure it

    The number is orders from homepage visits, by market and brand. The leading signals are time to first add-to-cart and the share of visits that end without an order. Every change ships against a holdout, with the sample size agreed with data science before it starts. The guardrails are what a homepage can quietly break: the mix of orders across verticals, first orders for new restaurants, refunds and complaints, and basket value, so a change that only moves people to pricier options shows up. Alongside the numbers, I'd talk to customers every month, starting with those who opened the app and left, because the reason behind a number decides the fix.

  8. AI that customers actually feel

    Your posting asks for someone who can tell real value from hype. DoorDash's own numbers help. On the Q3 2026 call, DoorDash put orders from third-party AI agents at maybe several hundred in total. So I'd put AI where customers already decide: carousels written for them, a strip that knows the moment, and for the undecided, a way to say what they fancy ("something light, under £15, fast") and get a short list. Ask DoorDash already does this from the search bar in parts of the US; the homepage question is when to offer it, and to whom, tested against today's homepage on time to order. Outside agents come later, and the catalogue behind the homepage should be ready for them. This is how I work. At MR Neo I made the whole company AI-native in under two months, with a central AI knowledge base, a sales AI workspace and automated team workflows. I'm leading its redesign into an AI-first platform for early 2027, and I designed flow-conflict detection for its automation builder. I built this city with Claude Code. I haven't run formal evals yet; that's what I'm learning next.

  9. Where it could go in 12 months

    By the time Deliveroo runs on the single stack, the homepage answers "what are you here for?" before the customer has to. The strip and the carousels follow the customer and the moment. A restaurant regular sees the shop that suits their meal. Each market gets the global capability in its own language and rhythm. And every slot shows because it's relevant to the person looking, whichever team wants it. On one stack, a homepage gain in one market can ship to all three brands.

  10. Why me, why Deliveroo

    The homepage is where a marketplace earns the next order, and the call it makes for each customer is the product problem I enjoy most. I've made it before. At GMNG my homepage and first-time redesign came from talking to players, and the engagement push I led as PM (more games, the 7-day poker loop, and changes such as pinned favourite games and faster loading) together tripled gameplay frequency and lifted revenue 50% in key games. GMNG grew to 1M+ users and $500k+ in deposits while I led a team of nine, and a two-tier referral scheme I ran brought in 200,000+ new users in a single month. I ran A/B tests and feature flags in Firebase and retention campaigns in CleverTap, and use SQL for product data. At XY ADS I built a self-service ad exchange from scratch: $100k a month in ad spend ran through it by the time I left. My first AI project was about food: a neural network trained on 10,000 images to recognise 100 foods for a calorie-tracking app, during my B.Tech in Information Technology at Manipal.
