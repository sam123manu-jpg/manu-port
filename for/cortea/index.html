  2. A finding is only worth something if the reviewer trusts it and still checks it

    Your agents read audit reports, statements, notes and working papers before sign-off, and helped review more than 4,000 audit reports last season. Every finding links to its source, and reviewers validate, override, comment on and approve it, all on the record. Your homepage promises "No hallucinations". Your Berlin auditor posting names the risk on the other side: preventing over-reliance on automated results. Between those two sit two ways a quality layer fails. Noise: a check that flags too much that turns out fine, until reviewers learn to click Accept. Silence: a check that misses an inconsistency, which looks exactly like a clean file. The first shows up in your product, in every override. The second doesn't, until a reviewer, a regulator or Companies House finds it. Your engineers are building eval pipelines and your methodology auditors write the checks' logic. The product job is to join them into one answer per check: how often it's right, what it misses, and whether reviewers still look.

  3. Three bets for the first six months

    Start with what reviewers already tell you. Every override and comment is a label. For each check, such as a disclosure checklist under FRS 102 or a figure that must tie across the statements and the notes, I'd look at how often its findings are accepted, why they're overridden, and how long a file takes to clear. If the reason for an override isn't captured yet, one tap for wrong, right but immaterial, or already handled would make each one useful. Each week I'd sit with a reviewer clearing a real file, to see where they open the source and where they click through. Bet one: recheck after a fix, if it doesn't happen already. When an auditor corrects a figure or adds a missing disclosure, the agent reruns that check and the checks that depend on it, such as the note that must tie to the primary statement or last year's comparative, before the finding closes. Bet two, with your designers: show what was checked, not only what was found. Where a check comes back clean, it says which requirements and cross-references it covered and which it didn't, so silence is never read as assurance. Bet three comes from the weekly visits, because the customers should pick it. I'd prototype each in Claude Code against public accounts filed at Companies House before engineering builds it, and measure findings accepted per check, misses found later, time to clear a file, and how often reviewers open the source before they accept.

  4. One gate for every agent change

    This is the area I'd go deep on: your evals and quality framework. Every change to a model, a prompt or a check's logic would pass the same gate before a firm sees it. A fixed set of files with known review points, built from public filed accounts and cases your methodology auditors write, so no client data moves, plus seeded errors: a disclosure removed, a figure changed in one note but not in the statement. Run it before and after the change. A change that loses a known catch doesn't ship, even if it flags more. Each pattern of overrides, and each miss found later, becomes a new case your methodology auditors write up, so the set grows from real failures. Overrides can't show misses, so with pilot firms' agreement an experienced auditor would review a sample of signed-off files blind and count what the agents missed. That's how I'd put numbers behind "No hallucinations" that a partner can read. The failure modes I'd watch: a check that is right but immaterial, which teaches reviewers to skim, and a model upgrade that rewords findings so reviewers stop recognising them.

  5. I've been the quality layer on AI findings before

    MR Neo is a CRM and automation platform with 60+ pages and, when I joined, no testing process. I recorded 4–5 hours of myself testing it and thinking aloud, what I click and why, and turned that into Claude skills built on existing bug-finding skills. They found 588 bugs and UX issues. I reviewed every finding myself before it reached the developers' board, so only the ones I approved went up. When the developers said something was fixed, my fix-check skill retested it against the original report, checked what sits next to the fix for anything it broke, and marked it Repeated if it still failed. Anything new it found came back to me as a candidate, not straight to the board. The team now uses and improves these skills. I also designed flow-conflict detection for MR Neo's automation builder, so users see which flows overlap, contradict or supersede each other before they break: a cross-reference check, like a note that has to agree with the statement.

  6. Lará, my AI side project

    Lará is an AI platform that runs a hotel and looks after its guests, which I'm prototyping with AI coding tools. The proof of concept is built and I'm preparing the pitch to a first hotel. It has an AI concierge that books services, an AI staff assistant that answers from the hotel's own procedures, and AI reply drafts in Portuguese, English, French and German. The decision I'm proudest of is where the AI stops: allergy alerts never depend on it. They reach the restaurant team and manager within seconds, must be acknowledged, and work with AI switched off. I built it in seven phases, each with a security attack-and-harden pass, and it runs 260 automatic database security tests. That's the line your posting asks for: what's worth building, what's not ready, and where humans stay in the loop.

  7. Where it could go in 12 months

    Every check has a record a partner can read before relying on it: how it performed on files like theirs, what it covers, and what it doesn't. A firm that adds its own checklist, or a standard that changes, goes through the same gate as your own changes. Reviewers spend their time on findings that need judgement, and a recheck closes the rest. And the record becomes part of the sale: when firms compare Cortea with tools built in-house, you can show how each check performs, not only say it.
