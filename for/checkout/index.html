  2. Where I'd look first: the front door to Legal

    Most legal work at a payments company arrives as a request from the business: a merchant agreement with a non-standard clause, a vendor contract, an NDA, a question about which terms apply in a market. Your legal centre shows how much each answer depends on context: eleven sets of country terms, each with its own Checkout.com entity and most with their own regulator, from the FCA in the UK and the ACPR in France to the Central Bank of the UAE, MAS in Singapore and the Central Bank of Brazil. Intake already runs through Jira Service Management and knowledge sits in Confluence, which is a good base: the data to learn from already exists. My guess at the friction, to check first: requests that arrive missing what a lawyer needs (which entity, the counterparty, the value, the deadline), so the first reply is a question; questions answered again because the last answer is hard to find; and low-risk contracts waiting in the same queue as complex ones. You've already shown the payoff where a review follows clear rules: your results say AI-driven policy reviews cut due diligence time by 83%. Much of intake has that shape too.

  3. What I'd build first

    Discovery comes first. I'd pull a quarter of intake tickets from Jira, tag them by type, and sit with a commercial lawyer, a privacy lawyer and a risk partner through real requests. For each type: how often it comes, how long it waits, how many back-and-forths before work starts, and what a wrong answer would cost. That gives the pipeline, ranked by user need, value, feasibility, adoption and risk, and Legal and Enterprise Risk leadership sees the ranking as the choice it is: a named problem and a decision, not a tool looking for a use. Then three steps, built with Legal Operations, who run these tools today. Each is prototyped on anonymised past requests first, then piloted with one team before it spreads. One, a smarter front door: the intake form asks for what the lawyer will need for that type of request, AI drafts the summary and suggests the queue, and a person routes it. Two, answers with sources: common questions are answered from approved Confluence pages and playbooks, every answer cites the page it came from, and when no approved page covers the question it says so and opens a ticket. Lawyers own the pages, so fixing an answer means fixing the page. Three, a standard-paper lane, where one doesn't exist yet: an NDA or low-risk contract on Checkout.com's own paper is checked against the lawyer's playbook, deviations are flagged, and a lawyer signs off. Each step is documented and handed to a named owner before the next starts. What I'd measure: time to first response and cycle time for each request type, back-and-forths before work starts, the share of questions answered from sources without a ticket, checked for accuracy by lawyers on a sample, and whether people come back to use it.

  4. How I'd keep it safe

    Legal work is privileged, confidential and full of personal data, so the controls are agreed before the pilot, not after. With Information Security, Data Protection and Procurement: which tool may see which data, and a review of each AI vendor like any other supplier, covering where data sits, how long it's kept and that it isn't used for training. Answers inherit the permissions of their source, so the assistant never shows someone a page they couldn't open, and privileged matters stay out of anything the wider business can query. AI drafts and people decide: nothing reaches a counterparty or a regulator without a lawyer. And test before trust: a question set built from real past requests, including ones it should refuse, with the right answer agreed by a lawyer, run against every change before it ships. A confident wrong answer counts as worse than "I can't find that". Each week a lawyer checks a sample of live answers, and every mistake becomes a new test case.

  5. Getting lawyers building

    The fastest way I know to get people building is to start from their own judgement. At MR Neo I recorded four to five hours of myself testing the platform and thinking aloud, turned it into reusable AI skills, and handed them to the team, who now use them and keep improving them. For Legal and Enterprise Risk the same move works: a lawyer's review checklist becomes a playbook tool with an owner, worked examples and test cases. I'd run hands-on sessions where everyone leaves with one working tool for their own work, write short guides for the patterns that keep coming up, keep a shared library of prompts, prototypes and reusable pieces with an owner for each, and hold a regular builders' forum where people show what worked and what didn't. The best tools get hardened and supported as products; the rest stay personal. I'd know it's working when lawyers bring me ideas they've already prototyped.

  6. Where it could go in 12 months

    Legal and Enterprise Risk works from one source of truth. The business asks first and gets a sourced answer or a well-formed ticket; lawyers spend their time on judgement, not on chasing facts; standard contracts move in a lane of their own; and the builder community has turned its best prototypes into tools other teams use, each with an owner and a test set. Every tool is measured the same way, on time saved, cycle time, quality, satisfaction and adoption, so leadership decides where to invest next from the pipeline, not from a demo. Enterprise Risk gets the same front door for risk assessments and policy questions, starting with whatever discovery ranks highest. And the same pattern can reach matter management, contract reporting and regulatory tracking across your licensed entities, with Compliance, Technology and Data as partners.

Read-only access was my call from day one. On Lará, the AI hotel app I'm prototyping with Claude Code, the staff assistant answers from the hotel's own procedures, every build phase had a security attack-and-harden pass, and allergy alerts never depend on the AI: they reach the restaurant team and manager within seconds and work with AI switched off. At XY ADS I turned partners' API documents into the exact work our developers had to build, the same translation between experts and engineers this role needs.
